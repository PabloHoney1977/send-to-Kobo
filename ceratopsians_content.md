# Ceratopsians: The Horned Dinosaurs

# Ceratopsia

Ceratopsia (from Greek, meaning "horned faces") is a group of herbivorous dinosaurs that lived primarily during the Cretaceous Period, with origins extending back into the Late Jurassic. They are characterised by a rostral bone at the tip of the snout forming a parrot-like beak, and in many species elaborate horns and a bony frill extending from the back of the skull. The group ranges in size from the small, frilled but hornless *Protoceratops* (about 2 metres long) to the massive *Triceratops*, which reached lengths of 9 metres and weighed up to 12 tonnes.

## Classification and Phylogeny

Ceratopsia belongs to the broader clade Marginocephalia, which it shares with the thick-skulled Pachycephalosauria. Within Ceratopsia, the major division is between:

- **Psittacosauridae** — small, bipedal to quadrupedal early ceratopsians with a well-developed rostral beak but no frill or horns. The genus *Psittacosaurus* is the most species-rich non-avian dinosaur genus known.
- **Neoceratopsia** — all more derived ceratopsians, including:
  - **Leptoceratopsidae** — small, lightly built forms retaining some bipedal capability.
  - **Protoceratopsidae** — medium-sized quadrupeds with frills but modest or absent horns.
  - **Ceratopsidae** — the large-bodied, fully quadrupedal horned ceratopsians of North America and Asia, divided into:
    - **Centrosaurinae** — typically with a single large nasal horn and elaborate frill ornaments (*Centrosaurus*, *Styracosaurus*, *Pachyrhinosaurus*).
    - **Chasmosaurinae** — typically with elongated frills and developed brow horns (*Chasmosaurus*, *Pentaceratops*, *Triceratops*, *Torosaurus*).

## Anatomy

### Skull and Frill

The most distinctive feature of ceratopsians is the cephalic ornament. The rostral bone — unique to ceratopsians — forms a sharp, keratinous beak ideal for cropping tough vegetation. The frill is formed by elongation of the parietal and squamosal bones of the skull. In centrosaurines the frill tends to be shorter and richly ornamented with epiossifications (small bony knobs or hooks around the margin), while in chasmosaurines the frill is often enormously elongated and fenestrated (containing large open windows covered in life by skin).

Horns are ossified cores covered in keratin, much like those of modern bovids. Ceratopsids typically bear a nasal horn and two brow (supraorbital) horns, though the relative size of these varies enormously between species and may have varied within species by age and sex.

### Postcranial Skeleton

Ceratopsids were fully quadrupedal with a distinctive upright hindlimb posture and a more sprawling, semi-erect forelimb. The forelimbs were robust and heavily muscled to support the massive skull. The feet bore hooves rather than claws, consistent with a ground-dwelling herbivorous lifestyle.

### Size

Adult ceratopsids represent some of the largest terrestrial animals of the Cretaceous. *Triceratops* is among the largest, with skulls exceeding 2.5 metres in length — among the largest skulls of any terrestrial animal. Even mid-sized ceratopsids such as *Centrosaurus* exceeded 6 metres in length and several tonnes in weight.

## Palaeobiology

### Diet

All ceratopsians were herbivores. The sharp beak was used for cropping plant material, and the teeth — organised in batteries of closely packed dental columns — could process tough, fibrous vegetation such as cycads, palms, and ferns. Unlike hadrosaurs, ceratopsids could not grind food side-to-side; instead they sheared vegetation with a vertical, scissor-like jaw action.

### Herding and Social Behaviour

Bone beds — accumulations of many individuals of the same species — are known for several centrosaurine ceratopsians, including *Centrosaurus*, *Styracosaurus*, and *Pachyrhinosaurus*. These deposits, sometimes containing thousands of individuals, are interpreted as evidence of herding behaviour, with mass deaths potentially caused by flooding during seasonal migrations. Chasmosaurines are less commonly found in bone beds, though *Triceratops* remains are often found singly.

### Frill and Horn Function

The function of the elaborate frill and horn array has been debated extensively. Proposed functions include:

- **Intraspecific display and species recognition** — frills and horns differ markedly between closely related species, supporting their role in identification and mate selection.
- **Thermoregulation** — the heavily vascularised frill may have helped regulate body temperature.
- **Defence** — large brow horns could have been used against predators such as *Tyrannosaurus rex*, with which *Triceratops* was contemporaneous.
- **Combat** — healed bite and horn-impact injuries on frill bones and horn cores suggest intraspecific combat, similar to modern ungulates.

Isotopic and histological studies of frill bone suggest active blood flow, supporting both thermoregulation and display functions.

## Distribution and Fossil Record

Ceratopsians originated in Asia, where the earliest forms — psittacosaurids and protoceratopsids — are well-represented in the Cretaceous of Mongolia and China. Large-bodied ceratopsids diversified primarily in Laramidia (western North America), where they are among the most abundant large herbivores in Late Cretaceous formations including the Judith River, Dinosaur Park, and Hell Creek Formations. A small number of ceratopsid genera are also known from Asia.

The last ceratopsians, including *Triceratops* and *Torosaurus*, survived until the end-Cretaceous mass extinction approximately 66 million years ago.

# Triceratops

*Triceratops* (meaning "three-horned face") is the most iconic of all ceratopsian dinosaurs, and one of the most recognisable of all dinosaurs. It lived during the very end of the Late Cretaceous Period (Maastrichtian stage, approximately 68–66 million years ago) in what is now western North America. *Triceratops* is among the last non-avian dinosaurs to have evolved and was one of the final dinosaur genera to become extinct at the K-Pg boundary.

## Discovery and Naming

The first material attributed to *Triceratops* was a pair of brow horns attached to a partial skull, discovered in 1887 near Denver, Colorado, by George Lyman Cannon. These were initially described by Othniel Charles Marsh as the horns of a giant bison (*Bison alticornis*). Further, more complete material collected in 1888 by John Bell Hatcher in Wyoming led Marsh to recognise the animal as a new genus of dinosaur, which he named *Triceratops horridus* in 1889. The species name *horridus* comes from the Latin for "rough" or "rugged," referring to the texture of the fossil bones.

Since then, *Triceratops* has become one of the most extensively collected large dinosaurs, with over 50 skulls and numerous postcranial elements known.

## Species

Two species of *Triceratops* are currently recognised as valid:

- ***Triceratops horridus*** — described by Marsh in 1889; characterised by relatively shorter, more robust brow horns and a rounder frill.
- ***Triceratops prorsus*** — described by Marsh in 1890; characterised by longer, more forward-curving brow horns and a narrower snout.

Some researchers have proposed that *T. horridus* and *T. prorsus* represent growth stages or sexual morphs of a single species, while others maintain two distinct species stratigraphically separated within the Hell Creek Formation, with *T. horridus* appearing earlier and *T. prorsus* later.

The closely related *Torosaurus* — distinguished by its enormous fenestrated frill — was proposed by John Scannella and Jack Horner in 2010 to represent the mature adult form of *Triceratops*, a hypothesis that remains controversial. Most palaeontologists currently treat them as separate genera.

## Anatomy

### Skull

The skull of *Triceratops* is among the largest of any land animal. In large adults it reaches 2.5 metres in length, comprising roughly one-third of the total body length. It bears three prominent horns: a relatively short nasal horn above the snout and two large brow (supraorbital) horns above the eyes, which can reach over a metre in length in adult individuals. The frill is solid bone (unlike the fenestrated frills of chasmosaurines such as *Chasmosaurus*), oval to round in outline, and edged with small epiossifications called epoccipitals.

Bone histology and surface texture of the frill suggest it was covered by skin that was richly supplied with blood vessels, consistent with a display or thermoregulatory function. Some specimens show healed injuries on the frill and horn cores consistent with intraspecific combat.

### Body

*Triceratops* was a massive quadruped estimated at 7.9–9 metres in total length and 6–12 tonnes in mass, making it one of the heaviest ceratopsids. The forelimbs were columnar and heavily built; debate has existed over whether the forelimbs were held in a fully erect or slightly sprawling posture. Trackways and biomechanical analysis suggest a slightly outward-angled forelimb posture, intermediate between fully sprawling and fully erect. The hindlimbs were fully erect and powerful.

The tail was relatively short and stiff. The body was barrel-shaped, consistent with a large fermentation chamber for processing fibrous plant material.

## Palaeobiology

### Diet and Feeding

*Triceratops* was a low browser, feeding on ground-level and low-growing vegetation. The strong beak, combined with shearing dental batteries, allowed it to process tough fibrous plants including palms, cycads, and ferns that were abundant in the Hell Creek environment. Unlike hadrosaurs, which had sophisticated grinding dentition, ceratopsids sheared their food and likely relied on gut fermentation for further digestion.

### Interaction with *Tyrannosaurus*

*Triceratops* was contemporaneous with *Tyrannosaurus rex* in the Hell Creek Formation, and fossil evidence of their interactions has been found. Several *Triceratops* skulls bear bite marks consistent with *Tyrannosaurus* teeth, including on the brow horns and frill. Some of these marks show bone remodelling indicating the animal survived the attack. One specimen shows a brow horn that was bitten off and regrew, demonstrating that *Triceratops* could survive significant injury. The horns and solid frill of *Triceratops* were likely effective defensive weapons and armour against *Tyrannosaurus*.

### Horn Function

The large brow horns of *Triceratops* are generally interpreted as serving multiple functions: defence against predators, intraspecific combat between rivals, and display for mate attraction. The significant sexual dimorphism suggested by some studies (with individuals possibly differing in horn curvature and frill shape) supports a display or combat function. Experimental biomechanical studies suggest the horns could withstand substantial forces consistent with locking and pushing during combat.

## Habitat

*Triceratops* lived in a subtropical to warm-temperate coastal plain environment. The Hell Creek Formation preserves evidence of a diverse ecosystem including forests of conifers, angiosperms, and ferns, interspersed with rivers and flood plains. Associated fauna included *Tyrannosaurus*, ankylosaurs, hadrosaurs, and a variety of small mammals, lizards, and crocodilians.

# Styracosaurus

*Styracosaurus* (meaning "spiked lizard") is one of the most spectacularly ornamented of all ceratopsians, distinguished by an array of long spikes projecting from the margins of its frill. It lived approximately 75–74 million years ago during the Campanian stage of the Late Cretaceous, in what is now Alberta, Canada, and Montana, USA.

## Discovery and Naming

*Styracosaurus albertensis* was first described in 1913 by Lawrence Lambe of the Geological Survey of Canada, based on a nearly complete skull and associated postcrania recovered from the Belly River Formation (now Dinosaur Park Formation) of Alberta. The species name *albertensis* honours the province of Alberta where it was found. Further material has subsequently been collected from the same formation, including a remarkable bone bed in Dinosaur Provincial Park.

## Anatomy

### Skull

The skull of *Styracosaurus* is immediately recognisable by the combination of a large, prominent nasal horn — typically 50–60 cm in length — and a frill bearing four to six large posterior spikes, the longest of which can reach 55 cm. Smaller spikes and epiossifications are present along the lower margins of the frill. The brow horns above the eyes are small and rounded, contrasting strongly with the prominent nasal horn — the inverse of the pattern seen in *Triceratops*.

The function of the elaborate frill spikes is debated. Species recognition and mate attraction are the most commonly invoked explanations, as the specific spike pattern differs between *Styracosaurus* and closely related centrosaurines such as *Centrosaurus*. Display to conspecifics would explain why such energetically costly structures evolved and were maintained.

### Body Size

*Styracosaurus* was a large ceratopsian, estimated at approximately 5.5–5.8 metres in length and weighing around 2.7–3 tonnes. It was somewhat smaller than *Triceratops* but comparable in size to *Centrosaurus* and other centrosaurines.

## Herding

The bone bed in Dinosaur Provincial Park associated with *Styracosaurus* contains the remains of numerous individuals of varying ages, from juveniles to adults. This assemblage is interpreted as a herd that perished together, possibly in a flood during a river crossing, a behaviour documented in modern wildebeest and other migratory ungulates. The presence of juveniles alongside adults is consistent with mixed-age herding. This bone bed represents some of the strongest evidence for herding behaviour in any non-avian dinosaur.

## Palaeoenvironment

*Styracosaurus* lived in the warm, low-lying coastal plain environment of Laramidia, the western island continent of the Late Cretaceous. The Dinosaur Park Formation preserves a diverse fauna of large dinosaurs including hadrosaurs (*Parasaurolophus*, *Lambeosaurus*), ankylosaurs (*Euoplocephalus*), tyrannosaurids (*Gorgosaurus*), and other ceratopsians (*Centrosaurus*, *Chasmosaurus*). The environment was a mosaic of floodplain forests, river channels, and coastal wetlands with a warm, seasonal climate.

# Protoceratops

*Protoceratops* (meaning "first horned face") is a small, early ceratopsian dinosaur from the Late Cretaceous of Mongolia and China. It occupies a pivotal position in ceratopsian evolution, being more derived than psittacosaurids but less specialised than the large-bodied ceratopsids of North America. *Protoceratops* is one of the most abundantly known Cretaceous dinosaurs, with hundreds of specimens ranging from embryos to large adults.

## Discovery and Significance

*Protoceratops andrewsi* was described in 1923 by Walter Granger and William King Gregory, based on material collected by Roy Chapman Andrews' American Museum of Natural History expeditions to the Gobi Desert. The expeditions, which discovered the first confirmed dinosaur eggs in history, found *Protoceratops* to be the most common dinosaur in the Djadochta Formation of Mongolia. The species name honours Roy Chapman Andrews.

A second species, *Protoceratops hellenikorhinus*, was described in 2001 from the Bayan Mandahu Formation of Inner Mongolia, China, and is distinguished by a pair of small bumps on the nasal region.

## Anatomy

### Size

*Protoceratops* was a small dinosaur by ceratopsian standards, reaching approximately 1.5–1.8 metres in length and weighing around 62–180 kg depending on the individual. Despite its modest size, the skull was proportionally large relative to the body.

### Skull

The skull bears a well-developed rostral beak and a distinct but relatively modest frill formed by the parietal and squamosal bones. Unlike ceratopsids, *Protoceratops* lacks true horns; the nasal region may bear a slight ridge or bump but no horn core. The frill shows considerable variation between individuals, and some researchers have proposed that frill size and shape vary with age and sex, with larger-frilled individuals being males.

### Sexual Dimorphism

The extensive *Protoceratops* fossil record has enabled statistical studies of variation. Two distinct morphs are recognised in adult specimens: a narrow-frilled, narrow-skulled morph and a broader-frilled, wider-skulled morph. These are interpreted as female and male morphs respectively, making *Protoceratops* one of the best-documented cases of sexual dimorphism in a non-avian dinosaur.

## Famous Specimens

### The Fighting Dinosaurs

One of the most extraordinary dinosaur fossils ever discovered is the "Fighting Dinosaurs" specimen from Mongolia, first found in 1971 by a Polish-Mongolian expedition. This fossil preserves a *Protoceratops* and a *Velociraptor* locked in combat: the *Velociraptor*'s sickle-claw is embedded in the throat of the *Protoceratops*, while the *Protoceratops* grips the *Velociraptor*'s arm in its beak. The two animals appear to have been buried alive, probably by a collapsing sand dune. This specimen is preserved in the Mongolian National Museum of Natural History in Ulaanbaatar.

### Nesting and Eggs

*Protoceratops* eggs and nests have been found in Mongolia. The eggs are elongated and arranged in a ring pattern. Some clutches contain embryos, allowing comparison of embryonic and adult anatomy. Growth series from hatchling to adult are among the most complete of any non-avian dinosaur, providing insight into ceratopsian ontogeny.

## Behaviour and Ecology

*Protoceratops* was a herbivore of the arid Djadochta environment, feeding on the low-growing plants available in what was a semi-desert of sand dunes and ephemeral streams. It shared its environment with *Velociraptor*, the oviraptorid *Oviraptor*, the ankylosaur *Pinacosaurus*, and numerous small mammals and lizards.

# Pachyrhinosaurus

*Pachyrhinosaurus* (meaning "thick-nosed lizard") is a distinctive centrosaurine ceratopsian unusual among its relatives for lacking a true nasal horn; instead it bore a large, roughened mass of bone called a nasal boss on the top of the snout. It lived approximately 70–69 million years ago during the Campanian to Maastrichtian stages of the Late Cretaceous in Alberta, Canada, and Alaska, USA.

## Discovery and Species

Three species are recognised:

- ***Pachyrhinosaurus canadensis*** — the type species, described by Charles Sternberg in 1950 from the Horseshoe Canyon Formation of Alberta. The original specimen was a large skull with the distinctive nasal boss.
- ***Pachyrhinosaurus lakustai*** — described in 2008 by Philip Currie and colleagues, based on material from a remarkable bone bed at Pipestone Creek in northwestern Alberta. This species bears larger frill ornaments and a different configuration of bossing than *P. canadensis*.
- ***Pachyrhinosaurus perotorum*** — described in 2011 from the Prince Creek Formation of northern Alaska, representing one of the most northerly dinosaurs known from that era.

## Anatomy

### The Nasal Boss

The defining feature of *Pachyrhinosaurus* is the nasal boss — a large, roughened, somewhat spongy mass of bone in the position where other centrosaurines bear a nasal horn. Controversy has existed over whether *Pachyrhinosaurus* developed this boss throughout life, or whether the boss represents a broken and remodelled horn from an earlier growth stage. Histological studies suggest the boss grew directly as a boss without passing through a horned stage, though juvenile specimens of *P. lakustai* may show a small horn preceding boss development.

Some specimens also bear a boss above the eyes (the supraorbital boss), and additional bossing may occur on the frill. These bony masses are interpreted as the bases for large keratinous structures in life.

### Frill

The frill of *Pachyrhinosaurus* bears elaborate curved hooks and spikes, particularly in *P. lakustai*, where the frill ornamentation is among the most complex of any centrosaurine. The species-specific nature of this ornamentation strongly supports a display and species-recognition function.

## The Pipestone Creek Bone Bed

The Pipestone Creek bone bed in Alberta is one of the most densely packed dinosaur bone beds in the world. It preserves thousands of *Pachyrhinosaurus lakustai* bones from individuals of all ages, interpreted as the result of a catastrophic mass mortality event, likely a flood that killed a migrating herd. The bone bed has been excavated over many years and continues to yield new material. It is the basis of the Philip J. Currie Dinosaur Museum in Grande Prairie, Alberta.

## Alaska Population

The *Pachyrhinosaurus perotorum* material from northern Alaska is particularly significant because it demonstrates that large ceratopsids lived well within the Arctic Circle during the Cretaceous. At that time, northern Alaska was at approximately 80° North latitude, though the climate was warmer than today. The presence of juveniles alongside adults in Alaskan deposits suggests that these animals either remained in the Arctic year-round or undertook long-distance migrations seasonally. Some researchers propose they migrated south during the polar winter when vegetation would have been unavailable.

# Centrosaurus

*Centrosaurus* (meaning "pointed lizard" or "sharp-point lizard") is a centrosaurine ceratopsian from the Campanian stage of the Late Cretaceous (approximately 76–75 million years ago) of Alberta, Canada. It is one of the best-known ceratopsians and is particularly notable for the extensive bone beds that preserve evidence of enormous herding behaviour.

## Discovery and Naming

*Centrosaurus apertus* was named by Lawrence Lambe in 1904 based on material from the Belly River Formation of Alberta. Subsequent collecting has yielded hundreds of specimens. Several other species have been named over the years, but most are now considered synonyms of *C. apertus* or separate genera.

## Anatomy

*Centrosaurus* was a medium-sized ceratopsian, approximately 5–6 metres in length and 1.5–2.5 tonnes in weight. It is characterised by a single large, forward-curving nasal horn and small, forward-pointing brow horns. The frill is short relative to chasmosaurines and bears a pair of large curved hooks at the midline of the posterior margin, along with smaller epiossifications around the perimeter. The exact form of the frill ornamentation is used to distinguish *Centrosaurus* from the closely related *Styracosaurus* (which has elongated spikes) and *Coronosaurus* (which has a different hook arrangement).

## Bone Beds and Herding

*Centrosaurus* is most famous for the massive bone beds in Dinosaur Provincial Park, Alberta, particularly the Hilda mega-bonebed which extends over an area of several square kilometres and is estimated to contain the remains of thousands of individuals. These bonebeds preserve evidence of mass mortality events interpreted as catastrophic flooding during seasonal migrations. The sheer scale of these deposits implies that *Centrosaurus* formed some of the largest dinosaurian herds known, comparable in size to the great ungulate migrations of modern Africa.

## Palaeoenvironment and Ecology

*Centrosaurus* shared the Dinosaur Park Formation with a rich fauna including the hadrosaurs *Corythosaurus*, *Lambeosaurus*, and *Parasaurolophus*; the ankylosaur *Euoplocephalus*; and the tyrannosaurid *Gorgosaurus*. Isotopic analysis of *Centrosaurus* tooth enamel suggests a diet focused on low-growing vegetation, with individuals potentially migrating seasonally in search of food across the coastal plain of Laramidia.
